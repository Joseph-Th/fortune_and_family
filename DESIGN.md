# Civic Dynasty

## Comprehensive Game Design Document

**Document type:** Game design only
**Genre:** Dynasty simulation, economic strategy, political strategy, social simulation
**Setting:** A late-medieval European-inspired city and its surrounding region
**Player role:** Founder and successive heads of a merchant or artisan family
**Primary fantasy:** Build a family institution that converts productive skill and commercial wealth into lasting civic power
**Working title:** *Civic Dynasty*

---

## 1. Executive Summary

*Civic Dynasty* is a single-player systemic strategy game about founding, expanding, governing, and preserving a powerful family inside a living medieval city.

The player begins with a household, a limited amount of capital, a trade, a few relationships, and no institutional protection. Through production, commerce, credit, property, marriage, patronage, guild membership, and political office, the family can rise from a small workshop to become one of the city’s ruling dynasties.

The game is not primarily about owning many buildings. It is about converting one form of power into another:

1. Skill and labor create goods and services.
2. Goods and services create income, reputation, and relationships.
3. Income becomes property, credit, information, and patronage.
4. These assets create influence inside guilds, courts, councils, churches, neighborhoods, and noble households.
5. Institutional power changes laws, contracts, taxes, licenses, and access to markets.
6. Those changes reshape the city and provoke rivals, dependents, reformers, workers, creditors, and outside authorities.
7. The dynasty must survive succession, scandal, crisis, and its own internal conflicts.

The intended campaign arc changes the player’s unit of decision over time.

- In the early game, the player manages a household and one business.
- In the middle game, the player manages a portfolio, a network of obligations, and a politically active family.
- In the late game, the player manages institutions, coalitions, public policy, regional finance, succession, and the stability of an urban order the dynasty helped create.

The design rejects a simple exponential accumulation model. Growth creates administrative burdens, political exposure, internal disloyalty, public resentment, and strategic dependence. A larger dynasty has more tools but also more surfaces on which it can be attacked.

## 2. Design Thesis

> A dynasty simulation about converting economic specialization into institutional power inside a living medieval city.

Every major system should reinforce this thesis.

A feature belongs in the game when it does at least one of the following:

- Creates meaningful economic interdependence.
- Gives people or institutions interests derived from their material position.
- Allows the player to convert one kind of power into another at a cost.
- Produces persistent consequences in the city.
- Creates a new class of decision as the dynasty grows.
- Makes family succession materially different from ordinary character replacement.

A feature should be removed or simplified when it exists only as repetitive activity, visual spectacle without systemic consequence, or a progression gate detached from the simulated world.

## 3. Core Player Fantasy

The player should feel that they are building a family institution rather than operating an avatar, a factory, or a city-state.

The central fantasy has six parts.

### 3.1 Productive competence

The family begins by being good at something concrete. It may bake bread, brew ale, forge tools, transport goods, lend money, practice law, operate an inn, manage estates, or trade cloth. Its first legitimacy comes from useful work.

### 3.2 Commercial intelligence

The player notices shortages, discovers reliable partners, identifies rising districts, understands seasonal demand, evaluates debtors, and places capital where it will produce leverage.

### 3.3 Social ascent

The family gains standing through quality, charity, marriage, education, religious participation, guild service, and public office. Social recognition is neither identical to wealth nor automatically purchased by it.

### 3.4 Institutional control

The family eventually influences who receives licenses, how debts are enforced, where roads are built, which guild rules apply, who receives city contracts, and how crises are managed.

### 3.5 Dynastic continuity

Children, siblings, spouses, cousins, wards, illegitimate descendants, and in-laws become the organization’s branches. Their talents and loyalties determine what the family can safely control.

### 3.6 Historical imprint

By the end of a campaign, the city should visibly and institutionally reflect the dynasty’s decisions. Districts have changed, laws have accumulated, industries have risen or collapsed, families have been displaced or elevated, and public memory contains stories about the player’s house.

---

## 4. Design Pillars

### 4.1 A living political economy

Prices, wages, production, consumption, credit, law, and politics are causally connected. Economic changes create political interests. Political decisions create economic winners and losers.

### 4.2 Power has multiple forms

Money, reputation, legitimacy, office, information, coercion, credit, property, kinship, and popular support are distinct. They can be converted, but every conversion has constraints and consequences.

### 4.3 Growth changes the game

Expansion increases strategic scope while reducing the viability of direct control. The player moves from operator to owner, from owner to patron, and from patron to institutional ruler.

### 4.4 People are not interchangeable modifiers

Characters have motives, relationships, obligations, competencies, memories, and claims. Important systems operate through people who may cooperate, bargain, defect, inherit, expose, or resist.

### 4.5 The city remembers

Major actions create durable effects. Property ownership, precedents, grudges, districts, public works, laws, debts, scandals, and family reputations persist across generations.

### 4.6 Information is a strategic resource

The player does not automatically know every price, intention, crime, relationship, or future shortage. Knowledge is acquired through business records, agents, gossip, office, guild membership, correspondence, investigation, and trusted relationships.

### 4.7 Late-game power creates late-game problems

The late game is not an extended purchase sequence. It is a struggle to preserve or transform an order under pressure from succession, debt, reform, external authority, rival coalitions, and public reaction.

## 5. Player Experience Goals

The game should regularly produce the following experiences:

- Discovering that a commercial problem has a social or political solution.
- Discovering that a political victory has created an economic vulnerability.
- Using family members to pursue parallel strategies that cannot be managed by one person.
- Building a chain of obligations rather than merely accumulating currency.
- Anticipating how a shortage will propagate through multiple industries and social groups.
- Choosing between efficient concentration and resilient diversification.
- Winning an office but inheriting its duties, enemies, and scrutiny.
- Facing a succession that exposes weaknesses hidden by a capable founder.
- Looking at the city decades later and recognizing the accumulated consequences of prior decisions.

The game should avoid making the player feel like:

- A courier moving characters between buildings.
- A production clerk repeatedly adjusting obvious quantities.
- A passive observer waiting for timers.
- A monopolist whose victory is guaranteed once early profits begin compounding.
- A politician clicking abstract influence buttons unrelated to material interests.
- A dynasty manager whose relatives are stat packages without independent stakes.

## 6. Scope and World Structure

### 6.1 Primary geographic scope

The campaign centers on one deeply simulated city with:

- Six to ten distinct urban districts.
- One or more suburbs outside the walls.
- A river, road junction, harbor, or comparable trade feature.
- A surrounding agricultural hinterland containing villages, estates, mills, mines, forests, and transport routes.
- Several abstracted external markets linked by regional trade routes.

The city is the main stage. Regional systems exist to create supply, demand, risk, and late-game expansion without requiring several equally detailed cities.

### 6.2 Why one primary city

A single city permits:

- Persistent social memory.
- Legible political coalitions.
- Recognizable districts and families.
- Detailed property relations.
- Meaningful local monopolies.
- Institutional continuity across generations.
- A strong sense that the player is changing a particular place.

Geographic expansion occurs through estates, trade privileges, branch houses, diplomatic missions, contracts, and financial interests. The design does not require the player to directly operate every distant workshop.

### 6.3 Population abstraction

The city contains two population layers.

**Notable characters** are individually simulated. They include family members, rival dynasties, officeholders, guild leaders, clergy, major merchants, master artisans, criminal figures, judges, creditors, and other socially consequential people.

**Ordinary households** are simulated as grouped economic and social units. They consume goods, supply labor, pay rent, move between districts, respond to prices, develop loyalties, and participate in unrest. Selected members can be promoted into notable characters when events make them important.

This preserves economic scale without requiring every resident to receive full narrative simulation.

## 7. Time Model and Campaign Length

### 7.1 Time scale

Time advances continuously with pause and multiple speeds. The design is organized around days, seasons, years, and generations.

- Daily time governs deliveries, work shifts, local prices, appointments, and short-term incidents.
- Weekly time governs wages, market cycles, political meetings, credit obligations, and household consumption.
- Seasonal time governs harvests, fairs, construction, disease risk, migration, and trade conditions.
- Annual time governs taxes, elections, apprenticeship progress, education, inheritance planning, and long contracts.
- Generational time governs succession, reputation inheritance, family branching, and institutional transformation.

### 7.2 Campaign length

A full campaign should normally cover fifty to eighty in-game years and two to four generations.

The player may continue indefinitely after achieving a formal legacy outcome, but the designed campaign should reach a meaningful conclusion before systemic repetition dominates.

### 7.3 Pace controls

The game pauses automatically or slows for decisions with lasting consequences, such as:

- Marriage contracts.
- Major loans.
- Elections.
- Trials.
- Succession settlements.
- Political crises.
- Guild charters.
- War contracts.
- Large acquisitions.
- Public scandals.

Routine operations continue under policies and delegated authority.

## 8. Campaign Structure

### 8.1 Foundation phase

The dynasty owns or rents one home and one modest business. The player performs close operational management and establishes a commercial identity.

Primary concerns:

- Cash flow.
- Input reliability.
- Product quality.
- Hiring.
- Household expenses.
- Basic reputation.
- Guild entry.
- Local relationships.

Typical decisions:

- Whether to buy cheaper inputs on credit.
- Whether to accept a demanding patron.
- Whether to employ a skilled worker with divided loyalties.
- Whether to reinvest profits or improve family living conditions.
- Whether to remain independent or seek a guild sponsor.

### 8.2 Establishment phase

The family owns several assets, employs specialists, and becomes visible to institutions.

Primary concerns:

- Delegation.
- Supplier and customer contracts.
- Credit.
- Property.
- Marriage.
- Apprenticeships.
- Guild politics.
- Neighborhood standing.

Typical decisions:

- Whether to vertically integrate.
- Whether to lease a business to a manager.
- Whether to finance another family.
- Whether to enter politics or remain commercially neutral.
- Whether to educate an heir for trade, law, clergy, administration, or diplomacy.

### 8.3 Ascendancy phase

The family becomes one of the city’s major interests. Its actions influence markets and public policy.

Primary concerns:

- Coalition building.
- Municipal contracts.
- Regulation.
- Rival containment.
- Public legitimacy.
- Family branches.
- Regional trade.
- Institutional appointments.

Typical decisions:

- Whether to support a price-control ordinance.
- Whether to use office to favor allied firms.
- Whether to expose a rival or preserve a useful balance.
- Whether to rescue an indebted guild.
- Whether to absorb a partner or keep them dependent.

### 8.4 Dominion phase

The dynasty has substantial institutional power but is vulnerable to systemic opposition and succession failure.

Primary concerns:

- Stability of the political order.
- Public debt.
- Noble or royal intervention.
- Reform movements.
- External trade shocks.
- Large-scale patronage.
- Dynastic unity.
- Inheritance.
- The legitimacy of concentrated power.

Typical decisions:

- Whether to constitutionalize family influence or rule informally.
- Whether to suppress, divide, or satisfy a reform coalition.
- Whether to bind the city to a crown, noble, league, or merchant federation.
- Whether to divide the family estate.
- Whether to preserve the city’s prosperity at the expense of family privilege.

### 8.5 Legacy phase

The player chooses or discovers what the dynasty will become.

Potential legacies include:

- Civic oligarchs.
- Merchant princes.
- Industrial monopolists.
- Bankers to kingdoms.
- Guild constitutionalists.
- Popular patrons.
- Ennobled landowners.
- Criminal sovereigns.
- Religious benefactors.
- Regional trade hegemon.

Legacy is evaluated through the condition of both the family and the city. A wealthy house presiding over a ruined city is a valid outcome, but not an unqualified success.

## 9. Core Gameplay Loop

The main loop is:

1. Observe conditions and acquire information.
2. Form a commercial, familial, or political plan.
3. Commit capital, labor, relationships, or reputation.
4. Operate directly or delegate through people and institutions.
5. Receive economic and social consequences.
6. Convert gains into a different form of power.
7. Defend the new position against obligations, rivals, and systemic reactions.
8. Reorganize the dynasty before the next phase of growth or succession.

The loop is intentionally broader than “produce, sell, upgrade.” Production provides the base from which higher-order play emerges.

## 10. Forms of Power

The player manages several non-fungible resources.

### 10.1 Liquid capital

Cash and easily sold goods. Used for wages, purchases, bribes, taxes, emergencies, and investment.

### 10.2 Property

Buildings, land, workshops, warehouses, homes, estates, and infrastructure rights. Property provides income, collateral, votes under some constitutions, neighborhood influence, and physical control.

### 10.3 Credit

The ability to borrow, extend loans, delay payment, guarantee contracts, and mobilize more capital than the family currently possesses. Credit depends on reputation, collateral, relationships, and political confidence.

### 10.4 Reputation

Public beliefs about quality, reliability, piety, generosity, ruthlessness, competence, and honor. Reputation is multidimensional rather than one universal score.

### 10.5 Legitimacy

The perceived right to exercise authority. Legitimacy comes from law, election, tradition, public service, religion, competence, and accepted social rank. It can be undermined by corruption, coercion, foreign dependence, and obvious self-dealing.

### 10.6 Influence

The ability to persuade or pressure particular people and institutions. Influence is relational and localized. Influence with the bakers’ guild does not automatically translate into influence with clergy or dockworkers.

### 10.7 Information

Knowledge about prices, inventories, debts, intentions, crimes, relationships, shortages, negotiations, and external conditions.

### 10.8 Kinship

Claims and obligations created by blood, marriage, adoption, wardship, sponsorship, and godparent relationships. Kinship provides access and continuity but also inheritance claims and internal political complications.

### 10.9 Coercive capacity

The ability to enforce outcomes through guards, watch officers, courts, criminal agents, debt seizure, or armed retainers. Coercion solves immediate problems but is expensive, visible, and legitimacy-damaging when overused.

### 10.10 Administrative capacity

The dynasty’s ability to coordinate assets, records, people, and contracts. Capacity comes from educated relatives, clerks, managers, procedures, offices, and trusted agents.

Administrative capacity is a principal limiter on expansion. Excess holdings without sufficient capacity generate waste, fraud, delayed decisions, and internal capture.

## 11. The Dynasty System

### 11.1 The family as the player’s organization

The dynasty is not a roster of controllable units. It is a network of people with shared identity and partially aligned interests.

The player acts as the recognized head of house, but authority depends on family law, custom, property control, age, legitimacy, and relationships.

### 11.2 Character attributes

Notable characters have:

- Age and health.
- Education.
- Trade skills.
- Administrative ability.
- Social ability.
- Legal and political knowledge.
- Risk tolerance.
- Ambition.
- Loyalty to house.
- Personal wealth.
- Reputation.
- Religious commitment.
- Relationships.
- Secrets.
- Claims.
- Personality tendencies.
- Current obligations.

Traits should modify behavior and options rather than serve as small universal bonuses.

### 11.3 Family roles

Family members may serve as:

- Heir.
- Business manager.
- Master artisan.
- Factor or trade agent.
- Estate steward.
- Guild representative.
- Lawyer.
- Clerk.
- Cleric.
- Political candidate.
- Courtier.
- Diplomatic marriage partner.
- Investigator.
- Household manager.
- Military contractor or officer.
- Unofficial fixer.

Roles create expertise, networks, and independent power bases.

### 11.4 Marriage

Marriage is a negotiated institutional bond rather than a romance minigame.

A marriage contract may define:

- Dowry.
- Bridewealth.
- Property rights.
- Inheritance rights.
- Residence.
- Business partnership.
- Debt guarantees.
- Political support.
- Education of children.
- Religious obligations.
- Conditions for annulment or widowhood.

Affection, compatibility, and personal ambition still matter because unwilling or alienated spouses may resist, form separate networks, or prioritize their birth family.

### 11.5 Children and education

Children develop through household conditions, tutors, apprenticeships, schools, universities, religious institutions, and fosterage with allied families.

Education is both capability development and network formation. A legally educated child gains access to courts and clerks. An apprentice raised in another house may form stronger ties to that house than to their own relatives.

### 11.6 Illegitimacy, adoption, and wards

The game supports nonstandard succession strategies.

- Illegitimate children may be acknowledged, hidden, educated, married, or excluded.
- Adoption can preserve a line or absorb a talented outsider.
- Wards create obligations and political leverage.
- Godparent and patron relationships create quasi-kinship networks.

These systems create alternatives without making inheritance friction disappear.

### 11.7 Family council

As the dynasty grows, major decisions may require or benefit from a family council. Members support policies according to their branch interests, beliefs, and expected inheritance.

The council becomes important when:

- The current head is weak or absent.
- Property is held jointly.
- Several branches control major assets.
- A succession is disputed.
- The family has adopted a formal charter.

The player can centralize authority, distribute it, bargain, threaten, or rewrite family arrangements. Each model has different resilience and efficiency.

### 11.8 Family branches

Adult children and siblings may form branches with their own residences, businesses, marriages, and ambitions.

Branches can:

- Extend the family into new industries and institutions.
- Provide redundancy.
- Compete for resources and succession.
- Conceal income.
- Defect politically.
- Become semi-independent allies.
- Split permanently into rival houses.

### 11.9 Succession

Succession is a central systemic event.

The death, retirement, incapacity, or removal of the house head triggers:

- Transfer of legal authority.
- Distribution or concentration of property.
- Review of contracts tied to the deceased.
- Reassessment of credit.
- Rival opportunism.
- Family council negotiations.
- Employee and client uncertainty.
- Potential legal disputes.
- Public judgment of the successor.

The player’s prior organizational design determines whether succession is routine, contested, or catastrophic.

### 11.10 Continuity models

The family may develop one of several governance models:

- Patriarchal or matriarchal command.
- Primogeniture.
- Partition inheritance.
- Family partnership.
- Elected house head.
- Branch federation.
- Trust-like common estate.
- Corporate charter under civic law.

No model is universally superior. Centralized structures are decisive but succession-sensitive. Distributed structures are resilient but politically contentious.

## 12. Household and Standard of Living

The family household is both a cost center and a source of status, education, health, hospitality, and political access.

### 12.1 Household expenses

Expenses include:

- Food.
- Clothing.
- Servants.
- Heating.
- Medical care.
- Tutors.
- Ceremonies.
- Hospitality.
- Religious donations.
- Security.
- Maintenance.

### 12.2 Standard of living

Higher living standards can improve health, education, marriage prospects, and elite access. They also increase fixed costs, expectations, visibility, and vulnerability to income loss.

A family that rises socially may find it difficult to reduce spending without losing legitimacy.

### 12.3 Residence

Homes affect:

- District relationships.
- Security.
- Household capacity.
- Hospitality.
- Prestige.
- Access to institutions.
- Exposure to disease, fire, or unrest.

The player may maintain several residences, but doing so creates staffing and administrative burdens.

## 13. Characters, Employees, and Labor

### 13.1 Employee categories

Businesses may employ:

- Unskilled laborers.
- Skilled workers.
- Apprentices.
- Journeymen.
- Masters.
- Clerks.
- Guards.
- Drivers.
- Buyers.
- Sales agents.
- Managers.
- Specialists.

### 13.2 Labor market

Workers respond to:

- Wages.
- Food prices.
- Housing costs.
- Workplace safety.
- Hours.
- Reputation of employer.
- Guild rules.
- Family connections.
- Religious calendar.
- Seasonal opportunities.

Labor shortages should constrain growth. The player may need to train apprentices, recruit migrants, improve conditions, poach talent, or lobby for policy changes.

### 13.3 Loyalty and organization

Employees can develop loyalty to:

- The owning family.
- A particular manager.
- Their craft guild.
- Their neighborhood.
- Their religion.
- A political faction.
- Fellow workers.

This produces meaningful internal politics. A popular manager can become indispensable or dangerous.

### 13.4 Delegation

The player gives managers policies rather than constant commands.

Policy examples:

- Target inventory levels.
- Maximum input prices.
- Minimum product quality.
- Preferred suppliers.
- Wage posture.
- Credit terms.
- Customer priorities.
- Legal risk tolerance.
- Maintenance spending.
- Bribery authorization.
- Hiring criteria.

Managers interpret policies according to competence, incentives, personality, and loyalty.

### 13.5 Compensation

Employees may be paid through:

- Wages.
- Piece rates.
- Profit shares.
- Housing.
- Food allowances.
- Apprenticeship rights.
- Loans.
- Marriage support.
- Promotion promises.

Compensation design affects productivity, loyalty, cost predictability, and the employee’s ability to become an independent competitor.

### 13.6 Workplace conflict

Conflict may arise from:

- Late wages.
- Unsafe conditions.
- Excessive hours.
- Religious violations.
- Manager abuse.
- Hiring outsiders.
- Mechanization or work reorganization.
- Guild rule changes.
- Food-price shocks.

Responses range from quiet theft and absenteeism to petitions, collective refusal, sabotage, or riot participation.

## 14. Businesses

### 14.1 Business identity

Every business has:

- Location.
- Legal owner.
- Operating manager.
- Workforce.
- Equipment.
- Capacity.
- Inventory.
- Cash account.
- Contracts.
- Debts.
- Reputation.
- Customer base.
- Supplier relationships.
- License and guild status.
- Maintenance condition.
- Security.
- Accounting quality.

### 14.2 Ownership and control

Ownership and operation are separate.

The player may:

- Operate directly.
- Appoint a family manager.
- Hire a professional manager.
- Lease the business.
- Form a partnership.
- Own a passive share.
- Use the business as collateral.
- Grant a relative beneficial ownership.
- Hold the property while another party owns the enterprise.

This permits economic influence without forcing direct control of every asset.

### 14.3 Business strategies

Businesses can compete through:

- Low price.
- High quality.
- Reliability.
- Speed.
- Location.
- Brand reputation.
- Exclusive contracts.
- Credit terms.
- Political protection.
- Guild privilege.
- Vertical integration.
- Illicit cost avoidance.

### 14.4 Quality

Quality depends on inputs, worker skill, equipment, time, supervision, and fraud.

Higher quality can:

- Raise prices.
- Improve reputation.
- Unlock elite customers.
- Reduce returns or failures.
- Satisfy guild standards.

Quality falsification can produce short-term margins but creates legal, reputational, and contractual risk.

### 14.5 Maintenance and capital wear

Buildings and equipment deteriorate. Deferred maintenance improves short-term cash flow but increases accident, fire, spoilage, and breakdown risk.

Investment can improve efficiency, quality, safety, capacity, or product range. Upgrades involve tradeoffs rather than a single linear tier.

### 14.6 Business failure

A business may fail through:

- Insolvency.
- Loss of license.
- Loss of master or manager.
- Input shortage.
- Fire or disaster.
- Reputation collapse.
- Debt seizure.
- Labor departure.
- Political retaliation.
- Contract penalties.

Failure does not always erase the asset. It may create an acquisition opportunity, a distressed lease, or a creditor dispute.

## 15. Industry and Production Chains

### 15.1 Design principles

Production chains should be deep enough to create interdependence but not so granular that the player spends most of the game routing minor components.

Each major industry should include:

- Several input sources.
- At least one meaningful substitute.
- Multiple output markets.
- Quality differentiation.
- Seasonal or logistical constraints.
- Regulatory or guild interests.
- Labor specialization.
- Political relevance.

#### Trade and transport

#### Hospitality and urban services

- Inns.

#### Finance and property

- Lending.

#### Luxury and prestige

- Jewelry.

### 15.3 Chain ownership

Owning an entire chain provides supply security, quality control, and internal coordination. It also creates:

- High capital requirements.
- Administrative load.
- Exposure to multiple markets.
- Political opposition.
- Fixed-cost risk.
- Potential anti-monopoly action.
- Vulnerability to disruption at critical nodes.

Independent contracting should remain competitive when relationships are strong and institutions enforce agreements.

### 15.4 Byproducts and substitution

Chains should produce secondary outputs and substitution opportunities.

Examples:

- Brewing creates spent grain useful for animal feed.
- Butchery creates hides for leather production.
- Poor grain may become ale rather than bread.
- Charcoal shortages may force lower-quality fuel use.
- Imported cloth may replace local weaving but harm local employment.

These connections allow shocks to propagate in varied ways.

## 16. The Economic Simulation

### 16.1 Economic actors

The economy consists of:

- Households.
- Businesses.
- Guilds.
- Landowners.
- Religious institutions.
- Municipal government.
- Noble or royal authorities.
- External merchants.
- Villages and estates.
- Criminal organizations.

Each actor earns, spends, saves, borrows, invests, and responds to changing conditions.

### 16.2 Household demand

Household consumption depends on:

- Income.
- Wealth.
- Household size.
- Social class.
- Season.
- Prices.
- Religion.
- District access.
- Reputation of sellers.
- Public events.
- Substitutes.

Demand is hierarchical. Poor households prioritize staple food, rent, fuel, and basic clothing. Wealthier households purchase quality, services, luxury goods, education, and status consumption.

### 16.3 Business demand

Businesses purchase:

- Inputs.
- Labor.
- Transport.
- Storage.
- Security.
- Legal services.
- Credit.
- Maintenance.
- Equipment.

A shock in one sector therefore affects many others.

### 16.4 Price formation

Prices emerge from:

- Local inventory.
- Expected demand.
- Supplier competition.
- Transport cost.
- Product quality.
- Contract terms.
- Market access.
- Taxes and tolls.
- Information quality.
- Hoarding.
- Regulation.
- Seasonal expectations.

The player should see causal explanations rather than only price movement.

Example explanation:

> Bread prices rose because the western harvest failed, two mills are under repair, the council delayed emergency imports, and several bakers are reserving flour for a military contract.

### 16.5 Contracts

Contracts reduce uncertainty but create obligations.

Contract types include:

- Fixed-price supply.
- Variable-price supply.
- Exclusive purchasing.
- Exclusive distribution.
- Municipal procurement.
- Military supply.
- Construction.
- Lease.
- Apprenticeship.
- Debt guarantee.
- Partnership.
- Insurance-like risk sharing.
- Forward delivery.

Contracts specify duration, quality, quantity, penalties, collateral, witnesses, and jurisdiction.

### 16.6 Inventory and storage

Goods have storage needs and may suffer:

- Spoilage.
- Leakage.
- Theft.
- Fire.
- Quality degradation.
- Storage fees.
- Inspection.

Storage is a strategic asset. Hoarding can stabilize operations, enable speculation, or provoke public anger.

### 16.7 Transportation

Transport requires time, labor, vehicles, animals, road access, security, tolls, and scheduling.

Transport constraints create local price differences and give political significance to roads, bridges, gates, docks, and patrols.

### 16.8 Seasons

Seasons affect:

- Harvests.
- Construction.
- River travel.
- Road quality.
- Heating demand.
- Disease.
- Festivals.
- Labor supply.
- Preservation.
- Trade fairs.

Seasonality creates predictable cycles that skilled players can exploit without making outcomes deterministic.

### 16.9 Business entry and exit

NPCs create businesses when they have capital, skill, opportunity, and legal access. They close, sell, lease, or default when conditions deteriorate.

This prevents the economy from becoming a static set of permanent producers.

### 16.10 Economic shocks

## 29. Events and Crises

### 29.1 Event philosophy

Events should arise from simulation state whenever possible. Scripted events establish scenario identity, but most incidents should be generated from existing tensions.

### 29.2 Event structure

A strong event has:

- A discoverable cause.
- Affected actors with interests.
- Several response paths.
- Short-term and long-term consequences.
- A record in city memory when significant.

### 29.3 Crisis examples

#### Grain crisis

A poor harvest, mill breakdown, hoarding, and delayed imports cause bread shortages. The council considers price controls, import subsidies, requisition, or inaction. Each response benefits and harms different groups.

#### Banking panic

A major trade house defaults. Depositors demand funds, creditors tighten lending, property prices fall, and several firms become vulnerable to acquisition.

#### Succession dispute

A rival family head dies without a clear heir. Branches seek legal rulings, political support, and credit. The player may intervene for property, alliance, or strategic balance.

#### Guild revolt

Journeymen organize against entry restrictions. Large masters want repression, smaller masters fear wage pressure, and reformers see a political opportunity.

#### Urban fire

Fire destroys housing and workshops. Reconstruction creates contracts and speculation while displaced households demand relief.

#### Noble intervention

A regional lord claims the city has violated charter obligations. The crisis may be resolved through money, legal appeal, factional support, or military preparation.

### 29.4 Crisis escalation

Ignored problems evolve. A small shortage may become inflation, unemployment, unrest, political collapse, or external intervention.

## 30. Progression Without Simple Tiers

### 30.1 Capability progression

Progression unlocks new forms of organization and action rather than only larger numbers.

Examples:

- Direct bookkeeping becomes double-entry-like professional accounting.
- Informal purchasing becomes contracted supply.
- Personal lending becomes a credit network.
- One workshop becomes a leased portfolio.
- Neighborhood favor becomes a political machine.
- Family command becomes a chartered house council.

### 30.2 Knowledge progression

Knowledge comes through:

- Apprenticeship.
- Education.
- Hiring specialists.
- Travel.
- Books.
- Guild secrets.
- Institutional office.
- Experimentation.

Knowledge may belong to individuals, businesses, or the dynasty. A master’s death can therefore create capability loss unless knowledge has been institutionalized.

### 30.3 Social progression

Social advancement requires accepted behavior, relationships, and institutional recognition. Wealth alone cannot instantly erase low status.

### 30.4 Political progression

Political advancement is lateral and strategic. Different offices create different powers. A market inspector may matter more to a food dynasty than a prestigious ceremonial office.

## 31. Anti-Snowball Design

### 31.1 Administrative friction

Every controlled asset consumes capacity. Overextension causes:

- Waste.
- Theft.
- Slow response.
- Inaccurate records.
- Manager capture.
- Contract failure.

### 31.2 Political visibility

Large market share attracts:

- Regulation.
- Rival coalitions.
- Public scrutiny.
- Higher expectations.
- External taxation.
- Anti-monopoly action.

### 31.3 Portfolio exposure

Concentrated empires are efficient but vulnerable to sector shocks. Diversified empires are resilient but harder to manage.

### 31.4 Succession risk

Rapidly centralized growth may depend on one exceptional founder. Institutions, trained heirs, and distributed competence improve continuity but cost money and control.

### 31.5 Dependency

Powerful dynasties depend on creditors, managers, clients, officeholders, and external authorities. Those relationships can be attacked.

### 31.6 Public expectations

A family that presents itself as civic protector is expected to provide relief during crisis. Failure damages legitimacy more than it would for an openly self-interested house.

### 31.7 Rival adaptation

Rivals respond to the player’s strategy. They may avoid direct competition and instead attack licenses, suppliers, credit, succession, legitimacy, or coalition support.

## 32. Late-Game Design

### 32.1 Late-game principle

Late-game play must introduce different decisions rather than repeat early decisions at larger scale.

### 32.2 Institutional governance

The player manages:

- Council coalitions.
- Guild federations.
- Public debt.
- Regional treaties.
- City-wide infrastructure.
- Legal reform.
- Public order.
- Elite competition.
- External sovereignty.

### 32.3 Constitutional transformation

The player may attempt to change the city’s governing structure.

Examples:

- Expand guild representation.
- Restrict office to property owners.
- Establish a merchant senate.
- Create a hereditary magistracy.
- Strengthen noble oversight.
- Formalize neighborhood representation.
- Create independent courts.
- Establish a public bank.

Constitutional change requires a coalition and creates durable winners and losers.

### 32.4 Regional financial power

A late-game dynasty may finance governments, trade leagues, wars, or major infrastructure. Returns are high, but default becomes political rather than merely commercial.

### 32.5 Dynastic federation

The family may become too broad for personal rule. The player must choose how branches share assets, offices, information, and succession rights.

### 32.6 Historical crisis

Each campaign should include at least one late-game crisis capable of overturning the established order, such as:

- Sovereign debt repudiation.
- Major war.
- Religious schism.
- Plague.
- Constitutional revolt.
- Trade-route collapse.
- Peasant uprising.
- Foreign occupation threat.

The crisis is shaped by prior simulation state. It does not produce identical outcomes each campaign.

### 32.7 End-state tension

The player should face a final strategic contradiction:

- Preserve family privilege or preserve city stability.
- Centralize power or secure succession.
- Exploit debtors or prevent systemic collapse.
- Support the crown or defend civic autonomy.
- Reform institutions or suppress challengers.

## 33. Victory, Legacy, and Failure

### 33.1 Legacy evaluation

The campaign evaluates:

- Dynastic continuity.
- Family wealth.
- Institutional influence.
- Commercial achievement.
- Public legitimacy.
- Cultural or religious patronage.
- City prosperity.
- Inequality and unrest.
- Regional reach.
- Historical reputation.

No single score defines success.

### 33.2 Legacy outcomes

Possible formal outcomes include:

- **Merchant Princes:** dominate regional trade and civic finance.
- **Masters of the City:** control the governing coalition across generations.
- **House of Industry:** transform one sector into the city’s defining economic engine.
- **Civic Founders:** create durable public institutions and broad prosperity.
- **Noble Ascension:** convert urban wealth into hereditary aristocratic standing.
- **Hidden Sovereigns:** rule through debt, secrets, clients, and criminal influence.
- **Guild Commonwealth:** establish a durable guild-based constitutional order.
- **Bankers to Crowns:** become essential financiers of external rulers.

### 33.3 Partial success

A dynasty can survive without achieving dominance. It may become a respected regional house, a specialized craft lineage, or a declining but culturally significant family.

### 33.4 Failure states

The campaign ends when:

- The family has no eligible successor or continuation mechanism.
- The family loses all meaningful assets and support.
- The dynasty is legally dissolved or permanently exiled.
- The player’s house fragments beyond any recognized continuity.

Bankruptcy, imprisonment, loss of office, or temporary ruin are setbacks rather than automatic endings.

## 35. Interface and Information Design

### 35.1 Interface goals

The interface must make a complex simulation legible without reducing it to unexplained modifiers.

The player should be able to answer:

- Why did this price change?
- Who benefits from this law?
- Why does this person support or oppose me?
- Which businesses depend on this supplier?
- What happens if this loan defaults?
- Which family member can manage this responsibility?
- Why is this district becoming hostile?

### 35.2 Main views

#### City view

Shows districts, buildings, traffic flows, construction, public works, unrest, and visible prosperity.

#### Dynasty view

Shows family tree, branches, roles, claims, education, relationships, succession, and household finances.

#### Ledger view

Shows businesses, cash flow, contracts, debt, inventory, property, and performance by cause.

#### Market view

Shows supply, demand, inventory, price history, expected events, trade routes, and confidence of information.

#### Political view

Shows institutions, offices, powers, selectors, coalitions, proposed laws, obligations, and current disputes.

#### Relationship view

Shows favors, trust, fear, obligations, conflicts, and network connections.

#### Chronicle view

Shows major events, laws, construction, family milestones, scandals, and city changes across time.

### 35.3 Causal explanations

Every major metric should support a cause breakdown.

Example:

> Workshop profit declined by 18% because wool prices rose, one loom was under repair, and a former manager moved three skilled workers to a rival shop.

### 35.4 Forecasts

Forecasts include uncertainty and assumptions. They should improve with better records, skilled staff, and information networks.

### 35.5 Map overlays

Useful overlays include:

- Property ownership.
- Rent.
- Employment.
- District loyalty.
- Crime.
- Fire risk.
- Transport cost.
- Guild influence.
- Political support.
- Disease.
- Supply dependency.

## 36. Narrative and Presentation

### 36.1 Emergent narrative

The primary narrative comes from systemic events, relationships, and consequences.

### 36.2 Authored content

Authored content should provide:

- Scenario-specific institutions.
- Historical texture.
- Distinctive rival families.
- Major regional crises.
- Character dilemmas.
- Cultural practices.

Authored events should read simulation state and adapt their available choices.

## 37. Scenario Design

### 37.1 Scenario components

Each scenario defines:

- City geography.
- Constitution.
- Major industries.
- External ruler.
- Religious structure.
- Trade routes.
- Starting families.
- Social tensions.
- Major historical pressures.

### 37.2 Example scenario: Rivergate

Rivergate is a walled commercial city where a navigable river meets two overland routes.

Key features:

- Grain arrives from western estates.
- Timber and ore arrive from northern valleys.
- Cloth and luxury imports arrive by river.
- The merchant council and craft guilds compete for representation.
- A regional prince claims emergency taxation rights.
- The old bridge is privately tolled by a declining noble family.
- The southern district is growing rapidly but lacks sanitation and political representation.

Starting tensions:

- Food prices are rising.
- Small masters resent guild entry restrictions.
- The city treasury is indebted after wall repairs.
- Foreign merchants seek permanent privileges.
- Two major families are approaching contested successions.

### 37.3 Starting backgrounds

Possible starts include:

- Baker household.
- Blacksmith household.
- Innkeeper household.
- Cloth trader household.
- Rural estate steward.
- Scribe and legal household.
- Money changer.
- Transport contractor.

Each start provides a different initial network, risk, and route into power.

## 38. Example Playthrough Arc

### 38.1 Years 1–5: survival and reputation

The player begins as a baker leasing a small shop in a working district. Grain prices are stable, but the family lacks guild sponsorship.

The player chooses to produce reliable middle-quality bread rather than compete on the lowest price. A respected miller extends limited credit after the player guarantees repayment with household goods.

The family hires a skilled journeyman who improves quality but expects a path to master status.

### 38.2 Years 6–12: contracts and household growth

A military provisioning contract raises demand. The player can expand quickly by accepting debt or remain conservative.

The player accepts the contract, leases a second oven, and appoints a sibling as manager. The sibling develops strong ties with workers and begins making independent promises.

A marriage with a grain merchant family secures supply but grants the spouse’s family a claim on future business profits.

### 38.3 Years 13–22: guild politics and property

The player purchases the original bakery and several neighboring tenements. Rent income diversifies the family but creates tension when food prices rise.

The journeyman requests sponsorship as an independent master. Refusal may preserve labor capacity but damage loyalty and guild reputation.

The player supports the journeyman, creating an allied bakery. This reduces direct profit but expands the family’s coalition inside the bakers’ guild.

### 38.4 Years 23–35: civic influence

A harvest failure causes a grain crisis. The family has stored reserves and can profit heavily, provide relief, or combine both through a city contract.

The player supports imports and sells reserves at a moderate margin. Landowners oppose the policy, while poor districts and import merchants support it.

A family member wins appointment to the grain office. The office provides information and authority but creates scrutiny over the family’s storage profits.

### 38.5 Years 36–50: institutional power

The dynasty controls several bakeries, warehouses, loans, and rental properties. Direct operation is no longer practical.

A rival coalition accuses the family of monopolizing food supply and steering contracts. The accusation is partly true.

The player can sacrifice a manager, disclose records, reform the guild, buy political support, intimidate witnesses, or accept limits in exchange for legal protection.

### 38.6 Years 51–65: succession and constitutional crisis

The founder dies. The designated heir is a competent lawyer but lacks credibility with workers and guild members. A younger sibling controls the operating businesses and has broader popular support.

At the same time, the prince demands a new tax and threatens to suspend the city charter.

The player must settle family authority while leading a civic coalition. A centralized succession may secure fast action but trigger a branch split. A family compact may preserve unity but weaken control.

### 38.7 Final legacy

The dynasty may emerge as civic protectors who establish a public grain reserve and independent treasury, or as private monopolists who secure hereditary privileges under princely protection.

Both outcomes are powerful. They produce different cities and different historical judgments.

## 39. Balancing Principles

### 39.1 No universally dominant asset

Cash, land, office, reputation, and credit each solve different problems.

### 39.2 Every efficiency creates concentration risk

Vertical integration, centralized authority, exclusive contracts, and aggressive debt use should improve performance while increasing specific vulnerabilities.

### 39.3 Relationships need maintenance

Allies require benefits, recognition, and credible commitment. A coalition cannot be permanently purchased once.

### 39.4 Recovery remains possible

The player should be able to recover from bankruptcy, loss of office, scandal, or succession failure through retreat, renegotiation, branch support, or a new specialization.

### 39.5 Consequences should be delayed but attributable

Many decisions should mature over years, but the game must preserve a clear chain of causation.

### 39.6 Complexity should unlock gradually

The player begins with a limited number of relevant systems. New systems become important as the family gains assets, relationships, and institutional exposure.

---

## 40. Difficulty and Accessibility

### 40.1 Difficulty dimensions

Difficulty can adjust:

- Economic volatility.
- AI planning depth.
- Information uncertainty.
- Political competition.
- Succession harshness.
- Crisis frequency.
- Forgiveness of administrative overextension.

### 40.2 Avoid artificial difficulty

Higher difficulty should not primarily change hidden production multipliers or make rivals immune to ordinary constraints.

### 40.3 Simulation assistance

Optional assistance includes:

- Recommended operating policies.
- Automated accounting summaries.
- Highlighted causal chains.
- Succession warnings.
- Contract risk indicators.
- Simplified crime and evidence rules.
- Reduced number of simultaneous political offices.

### 40.4 Pausable decisions

The game should not punish players for reading detailed economic or political information. Critical decisions pause the simulation.

---

## 41. Replayability

Replayability comes from:

- Different starting professions.
- Different city constitutions.
- Different rival family combinations.
- Procedural economic conditions.
- Variable trade routes and resource bases.
- Different inheritance structures.
- Different external powers.
- Different late-game crises.
- Distinct ideological and institutional legacies.

The goal is not merely a different map. It is a different political economy.

---

## 42. Content Boundaries

### 42.1 Included

- Deep business and economic simulation.
- Family and succession.
- Urban politics.
- Guilds.
- Property.
- Credit.
- Law and crime.
- Religion and charity.
- Regional trade.
- Abstracted warfare impacts.
- Persistent city development.

### 42.2 Deliberately limited

- Direct tactical combat.
- Manual movement of every character.
- Detailed battlefield command.
- Full simulation of multiple equal-scale cities.
- Crafting minigames.
- Repetitive dialogue trees for routine transactions.
- Decorative interiors without systemic relevance.

### 42.3 Explicitly rejected

- Linear profession ladders as the primary progression system.
- Buildings that function only as passive income generators.
- Politics represented by generic influence points.
- Crime as consequence-free sabotage abilities.
- A late game consisting only of larger production chains.
- AI rivals receiving unexplained economic bonuses.
- Universal reputation values that ignore audience and issue.

## 43. Minimum Coherent Game

A smaller but complete first design scope should contain:

### 43.1 World

- One city.
- Six districts.
- One hinterland.
- Three external trade routes.
- Approximately eight major dynasties.
- Approximately forty other notable characters.
- Grouped ordinary households.

### 43.2 Industries

- Grain, milling, baking, and brewing.
- Wool, weaving, dyeing, and tailoring.
- Timber, charcoal, metal, tools, and construction.
- Inns, transport, warehousing, and trade.
- Lending and real estate.

### 43.3 Institutions

- Four craft guilds.
- Merchant guild.
- City council.
- Market office.
- Court.
- Watch.
- Treasury.
- Religious charity institution.

### 43.4 Dynasty

- Marriage.
- Children.
- Education.
- Family roles.
- Branches.
- Succession.
- Family council.

### 43.5 Political content

- Elections or appointments.
- Ten to fifteen substantive laws.
- Municipal contracts.
- Public works.
- Corruption and investigation.
- At least three crisis chains.

### 43.6 Campaign arc

- Foundation through second-generation succession.
- At least four distinct legacy outcomes.
- One systemic late-game crisis.

This scope is sufficient to test the full thesis without requiring every possible medieval industry.

## 44. Full Game Expansion Areas

After the minimum coherent game proves the core loop, expansion can add:

- More constitutions.
- More industries.
- Additional religious structures.
- Universities and learned professions.
- Maritime trade.
- Mining regions.
- Advanced banking.
- Noble estates and court politics.
- Multiple cultural settings.
- More complex external diplomacy.
- Plague and demographic recovery.
- Printing and information transformation in later periods.

Expansion should add new institutional interactions, not merely more production recipes.

## 45. Design Risks

### 45.1 Simulation opacity

Risk: The economy becomes complex but unreadable.

Response: Every major change needs causal explanation, source confidence, and drill-down views.

### 45.2 Excessive micromanagement

Risk: The player must constantly correct routine operations.

Response: Policy-based management, competent delegation, exceptions, and meaningful automation.

### 45.3 Weak character identity

Risk: Characters become interchangeable optimization pieces.

Response: Persistent motives, relationships, obligations, branch interests, and contextual traits.

### 45.4 Unstoppable snowball

Risk: Early profit guarantees victory.

Response: Administrative capacity, political reaction, succession, systemic exposure, and rival adaptation.

### 45.5 Arbitrary punishment

Risk: Crises feel like random destruction.

Response: Most crises emerge from state, show warning indicators, and provide several defensible responses.

### 45.6 Politics detached from economy

Risk: Elections become a separate minigame.

Response: Offices control concrete laws, budgets, licenses, contracts, and enforcement.

### 45.7 Family system detached from strategy

Risk: Marriage and children become cosmetic role-playing.

Response: Family members carry operational roles, institutional networks, property claims, and succession risk.

### 45.8 Late-game repetition

Risk: The player continues buying businesses after strategic victory is already assured.

Response: Shift decision scale toward institutions, constitutional order, regional finance, and legacy conflicts.

---

## 46. Design Validation Questions

During development, every major design review should ask:

1. Does year twenty require fundamentally different decisions from year two?
2. Can the player explain why a major price changed?
3. Does political office control something economically concrete?
4. Can a family member become valuable enough that their disloyalty matters?
5. Can growth make the dynasty less stable?
6. Can an AI rival pursue a strategy other than direct competition?
7. Can the player recover from a major setback?
8. Does a crisis reveal prior structural choices rather than ignore them?
9. Does the city visibly remember major actions?
10. Are routine tasks delegated while consequential decisions remain with the player?
11. Can two wealthy dynasties possess meaningfully different forms of power?
12. Is there a viable path that does not require owning every stage of production?
13. Can legal, religious, and social institutions constrain raw wealth?
14. Does succession test the organization built by the prior generation?
15. Is the final legacy evaluated through both family success and civic consequence?

## 47. Summary of the Intended Experience

*Civic Dynasty* begins with a household trying to survive through useful work. It becomes a game about contracts, property, credit, and guild standing. It then becomes a game about marriages, coalitions, law, and public office. Finally, it becomes a game about institutions, succession, civic order, and historical legacy.

The player’s dynasty should not simply grow richer. It should become embedded in the city.

Its bakeries feed districts. Its loans sustain merchants. Its relatives sit in courts and guild halls. Its buildings reshape neighborhoods. Its enemies remember old seizures and broken promises. Its workers organize around managers the family once promoted. Its political reforms alter who can vote, trade, inherit, borrow, and hold office. Its succession arrangements determine whether the organization survives the death of its founder.

The ultimate question is not only whether the family becomes powerful.

It is what kind of city that power creates, who pays for it, and whether the dynasty can survive the order it built.
